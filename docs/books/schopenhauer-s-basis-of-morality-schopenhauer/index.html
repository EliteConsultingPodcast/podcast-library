THE BASIS OF MORALITY: Compassion, Ethics & the Human Will — Arthur Schopenhauer (1903).

THE BASIS OF MORALITY by Arthur Schopenhauer is one of the most penetrating and challenging works in the history of moral philosophy. Originally published in the nineteenth century and presented here in the 1903 edition, the work explores a fundamental question: What is the true foundation of morality?

Rather than accepting conventional religious, social, or philosophical explanations of ethical behavior, Schopenhauer attempts to discover the underlying motive that makes one person act morally toward another. He challenges the idea that morality can ultimately be based on abstract rules, commands, rational principles, or self-interest. For Schopenhauer, the most important ethical phenomenon is compassion. Genuine morality, in his view, arises when the distinction between oneself and another becomes less absolute and the suffering or welfare of another person is experienced as something that matters directly. This idea is connected to his broader philosophical system, particularly his conception of the Will, the underlying force that drives living beings and produces desire, striving, suffering, and conflict. The work is also a sustained criticism of Immanuel Kant's moral philosophy. Schopenhauer respects Kant's enormous influence but argues that Kant's categorical imperative does not successfully explain the actual psychological foundation of moral conduct. Instead, Schopenhauer seeks an ethical principle grounded in human experience and motivation. This audiobook series follows Schopenhauer's argument through four major sections: the introduction, his critique of Kant's ethics, his attempt to establish the foundation of ethics, and his metaphysical explanation of the fundamental ethical phenomenon.

1. INTRODUCTION

The opening chapter establishes the philosophical problem that Schopenhauer intends to investigate. The central question is not simply what people ought to do, but why human beings sometimes act morally at all. Schopenhauer distinguishes between actions that merely conform to social rules and actions motivated by genuine ethical concern. He is particularly interested in the motive behind an action. An outwardly charitable act may look moral, yet its true motivation could be vanity, fear, social pressure, ambition, or the expectation of reward. For Schopenhauer, genuine morality requires something deeper. He therefore begins searching for an internal principle capable of explaining authentic moral behavior. The discussion introduces several recurring themes that dominate the rest of the work: compassion, egoism, malice, self-interest, suffering, and the relationship between the individual and others. Schopenhauer's approach is deliberately critical. He does not simply accept established moral doctrines because they are traditional. Instead, he asks whether they actually explain human behavior. The introduction consequently prepares the listener for a philosophical investigation into the psychological and metaphysical roots of morality.

2. CRITIQUE OF KANT'S BASIS OF ETHICS

The second section is the longest and most detailed part of the work. Schopenhauer turns his attention to Immanuel Kant, whose moral philosophy had profoundly influenced European thought. Kant argued that morality should be grounded in reason and universal principles. His famous categorical imperative requires individuals to act according to principles that they could rationally will to become universal laws. Schopenhauer recognizes the power and importance of Kant's philosophy, but he believes that Kant's moral theory ultimately fails to identify the true source of ethical motivation.A major part of Schopenhauer's criticism concerns the difference between an abstract philosophical rule and the actual experience of moral motivation. A person may understand that a particular principle should be universal, but understanding a principle does not necessarily explain why that person should feel compelled to act according to it. Schopenhauer argues that Kant's system places too much emphasis on abstract reason and insufficient attention on human psychology. He also examines the relationship between morality and concepts such as duty, obligation, law, punishment, reward, and self-interest. In Schopenhauer's view, these concepts can describe systems of social regulation without necessarily explaining genuine morality. The distinction between legal behavior and moral behavior becomes particularly important. A person may obey the law because of fear of punishment, while a moral person may help another person without expecting any external reward. Schopenhauer therefore asks what distinguishes truly ethical action from behavior motivated by calculation. He also challenges attempts to derive morality from rational self-interest. If a person helps another only because doing so ultimately benefits himself, Schopenhauer questions whether the action should genuinely be considered moral. Throughout the critique, he examines Kant's formulations in detail and argues that morality cannot be reduced to intellectual reasoning alone. The deeper issue is psychological: What moves a person to act for another person's sake? For Schopenhauer, the answer cannot ultimately be found in abstract commands.

3. FOUNDING OF ETHICS

After criticizing Kant's approach, Schopenhauer turns to the positive task of establishing his own foundation for ethics. Here he examines human behavior and identifies three fundamental motives that can influence people in their relationships with others:egoism, malice, and compassion. Egoism directs the individual toward his or her own interests. It is concerned with personal advantage, pleasure, security, status, and survival. Malice represents the opposite tendency: the desire to harm another person or derive satisfaction from another's suffering. Compassion, however, motivates an individual to respond positively to another person's suffering and welfare. For Schopenhauer, genuine morality arises from compassion. This is one of the central claims of the entire work. A truly moral action is one in which the welfare of another becomes a motive in itself, rather than merely a means toward personal advantage. Schopenhauer therefore examines different forms of ethical behavior, including justice and loving-kindness. Justice involves refraining from harming others. It establishes a moral boundary: the individual recognizes that another person has claims that should not be violated. Loving-kindness, or benevolence, goes further. It involves actively seeking the welfare of another person. Schopenhauer sees both as expressions of compassion, although they operate in different directions. Justice prevents harm, while compassion can inspire positive assistance. The chapter also examines the distinction between genuine compassion and apparent generosity. An action may outwardly benefit another person while being motivated by vanity, social recognition, fear, or personal gain. Schopenhauer is therefore concerned with the inner motive rather than merely the external result. This leads to one of his most important ethical principles: the moral value of an action depends fundamentally upon its motivation. The section also explores the relationship between compassion and the recognition of another person's suffering. To act compassionately, the individual must in some sense overcome the narrow boundaries of egoism and recognize the other as a being whose suffering matters. For Schopenhauer, this provides a more realistic foundation for ethics than purely rational commands.

4. METAPHYSICAL EXPLANATION OF PRIMAL ETHICAL PHAENOMENON

The final section takes Schopenhauer's ethical theory into deeper metaphysical territory. Having identified compassion as the fundamental ethical phenomenon, he now asks why compassion is possible at all. This question connects morality directly to Schopenhauer's larger philosophical system. According to Schopenhauer, ordinary human experience is dominated by the Will—a blind, restless striving that manifests itself throughout nature and individual life. Human beings constantly desire, pursue, fear, compete, and struggle. Because the Will expresses itself through countless individual beings, people ordinarily experience themselves as separate individuals pursuing separate interests. Egoism naturally follows from this condition. Each individual experiences his or her own needs immediately and the suffering of others as something external. Compassion represents a remarkable interruption of this separation. When a person genuinely experiences sympathy for another, the suffering of that other person becomes psychologically significant in a way that resembles one's own suffering. The strict boundary between self and other becomes less dominant. Schopenhauer interprets this phenomenon metaphysically. At the deepest level, he argues, the apparent individuality of beings does not represent the ultimate reality of existence. The same underlying Will manifests itself through different individuals. Compassion can therefore be understood as an ethical recognition that the apparent separation between individuals is not absolute. This provides the metaphysical foundation for his earlier ethical analysis. The chapter also explores the relationship between morality, suffering, asceticism, and the denial of the Will. These themes connect The Basis of Morality with Schopenhauer's broader philosophy, particularly his conviction that desire and striving are inseparable from suffering. The ethical life therefore involves more than obeying rules. It involves transforming one's relationship to desire, egoism, and the suffering of other beings.
