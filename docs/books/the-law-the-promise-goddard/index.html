THE LAW AND THE PROMISE: Master the Law of Success Through the Power of Imagination - Neville Goddard (1961).

The Law and the Promise by Neville Goddard is one of the most influential works in modern metaphysical thought and the Law of Attraction tradition. In this book, Goddard goes beyond theory and presents what he calls “the Law” of imagination as the sole creative force behind reality, while also offering “the Promise”—a series of profound mystical experiences that confirm the divine nature of human consciousness. The book blends psychological insight, spiritual mysticism, and practical application, guiding the reader to understand that imagination is not merely fantasy, but the very substance of reality itself. Through a series of short chapters, Goddard illustrates how thoughts, assumptions, and inner states shape external circumstances, and how deliberate mental living can transform one’s life completely.

Chapter 1. The Law: Imagining Creates Reality

In this foundational chapter, Goddard establishes the central principle of his philosophy: imagination creates reality. He argues that everything a person experiences in the outer world originates from an inner act of consciousness. The external world is not independent, but a reflection of the individual’s dominant assumptions and imaginative states. To change one’s life, one must first change the inner narrative. Goddard emphasizes that imagination is not passive daydreaming but an active creative force, capable of shaping events, relationships, and outcomes. The chapter invites the reader to take full responsibility for their inner world, as it is the true source of all external conditions.

Chapter 2. Dwell Therein

This chapter explains the importance of sustained mental residence in the desired state. Goddard teaches that it is not enough to briefly imagine a wish fulfilled; one must “dwell therein,” mentally living as though the desired reality is already true. By consistently occupying the feeling of the fulfilled desire, the subconscious mind accepts it as fact and begins to manifest corresponding circumstances. The key idea is persistence—remaining faithful to the imagined state regardless of external evidence. Reality, in this view, eventually conforms to the inner assumption.

Chapter 3. Turn The Wheel Backward

Here, Goddard introduces the concept of revising past events through imagination. He suggests that memory is not fixed, but can be altered by mentally rewriting past experiences. By “turning the wheel backward,” one can revisit moments of regret, failure, or pain and reimagine them in a positive or resolved way. This practice changes the emotional charge of past events and reshapes their influence on the present. Goddard teaches that since consciousness is the only reality, even the past is subject to revision, allowing individuals to free themselves from limiting histories.

Chapter 4. There Is No Fiction

In this chapter, Goddard blurs the line between imagination and reality. He asserts that all imagined states are real at the level of consciousness, even if they have not yet manifested physically. What we call “fiction” is simply unmanifested reality. Every imagined experience exists in a parallel dimension of awareness and will eventually externalize if sustained. This idea encourages readers to treat their inner visions seriously, as they are seeds of future experience. The distinction between real and imaginary is therefore an illusion of the senses.

Chapter 5. Subtle Threads

Goddard explores the unseen connections that link imagination and manifestation. He describes reality as being woven from “subtle threads” of thought, emotion, and assumption. These invisible patterns determine the structure of one’s life. Small, often unnoticed mental habits can produce large external consequences over time. This chapter highlights the importance of awareness, suggesting that even fleeting thoughts contribute to the shaping of reality. By refining inner states, one can reweave the fabric of experience itself.

Chapter 6. Visionary Fancy

This chapter distinguishes between idle fantasy and creative imagination. Goddard explains that “visionary fancy” is the disciplined use of imagination to create intentional states. Unlike random daydreaming, visionary fancy is purposeful and emotionally charged. It involves seeing and feeling oneself already living the desired reality. The practice requires vivid mental imagery and emotional involvement, which impresses the subconscious mind. Goddard encourages readers to cultivate this ability as a spiritual discipline.

Chapter 7. Moods

Here, Goddard examines the power of emotional states. He argues that moods are not passive reactions but creative forces that shape perception and experience. A person’s dominant mood determines what they attract and how they interpret events. By consciously choosing and sustaining constructive moods—such as confidence, gratitude, or fulfillment—one can influence external reality. This chapter emphasizes emotional mastery as a key component of manifestation.

Chapter 8. Through The Looking Glass

In this symbolic chapter, Goddard describes the world as a mirror reflecting inner consciousness. Like Alice stepping through the looking glass, the individual must realize that external reality is a reversed image of internal states. What is seen outside is always a reflection of what is assumed inside. To change the reflection, one must change the image within. This metaphor reinforces the idea that the external world has no independent power.

Chapter 9. Enter Into

Goddard teaches the practice of mentally entering desired states. Rather than wishing or hoping, one must fully inhabit the experience of the fulfilled desire. This involves imagining sensory details, emotions, and thoughts as if the goal has already been achieved. By “entering into” the state, consciousness aligns with it, and manifestation follows naturally. The chapter emphasizes experiential immersion as the key to transformation.

Chapter 10. Things Which Do Not Appear

This chapter addresses the invisible origins of visible reality. Goddard explains that all physical phenomena arise from unseen states of consciousness. Before anything becomes tangible, it exists as an invisible assumption or belief. The unseen realm is therefore more real than the seen, as it is the source of all manifestation. This teaching encourages trust in imagination over sensory evidence.

Chapter 11. The Potter

Goddard uses the metaphor of a potter shaping clay to describe human consciousness forming reality. The individual is both the potter and the clay, shaping their own experiences through imagination. The “clay” of life is malleable and responds to inner direction. By changing assumptions, one reshapes the entire structure of their world. This chapter emphasizes creative responsibility and spiritual authorship.

Chapter 12. Attitudes

Here, Goddard focuses on mental attitudes as determinants of life outcomes. An attitude is a consistent way of thinking and feeling about oneself and the world. These inner positions create predictable patterns in external reality. A changed attitude leads to a changed life. The chapter encourages readers to adopt attitudes aligned with their desired outcomes rather than their current circumstances.

Chapter 13. All Trivia

In this chapter, Goddard highlights how seemingly insignificant thoughts and assumptions accumulate into major life experiences. “All trivia” refers to small mental reactions, judgments, and beliefs that are often overlooked. Over time, these minor inner events shape major outer realities. The teaching emphasizes vigilance over one’s inner dialogue, as nothing in consciousness is truly trivial.

Chapter 14. The Creative Moment

Goddard identifies the precise moment of creation as the point of assumption. When a person fully accepts a thought as true, regardless of external evidence, creation begins. This “creative moment” is subtle but powerful. It is not effort or struggle, but a quiet inner acceptance of a desired state. Once this moment occurs, the external world must reorganize itself to reflect it.

Chapter 15. The Promise: Four Mystical Experiences

In the final chapter, Goddard moves beyond practical law into mystical realization. He describes “The Promise” as a series of four transformative spiritual experiences that reveal the true nature of the self as divine consciousness. These experiences include states of awakening, inner revelation, and unity with the divine imagination. Unlike the Law, which is used to shape external reality, the Promise concerns ultimate spiritual fulfillment and self-realization. Goddard presents these experiences as inevitable for all beings, occurring at different stages of spiritual evolution. The chapter concludes the book on a transcendent note, pointing beyond manifestation to awakening itself.

Conclusion

The Law and the Promise is both a practical manual and a spiritual revelation. Through its chapters, Neville Goddard presents a worldview in which imagination is the sole creator, consciousness is the only reality, and life is shaped entirely from within. The “Law” empowers individuals to consciously design their experience, while the “Promise” offers a glimpse into ultimate spiritual awakening. Together, they form a unified philosophy of creation and realization, urging readers to recognize their divine creative power and live from the assumption of already fulfilled desires.
